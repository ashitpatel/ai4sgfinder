# AI4SG Finder

[![DOI](https://zenodo.org/badge/DOI/10.5281/zenodo.23840133.svg)](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23840133)

An AI assistant that helps NGOs and public agencies choose the right **AI-for-social-good (AI4SG) allocation method** for a field problem. It interviews you one question at a time, classifies your problem into one of twelve mathematical archetypes, grouped into five families, and writes a recommendation covering why the method fits, what goes wrong without it, how to deploy it on paper or in Excel, and what the published evidence says.

It is a system prompt plus a reference file, packaged so it runs in **Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Google AI Studio, and other browser-based assistants**, or through the Claude, OpenAI, and Gemini APIs.

## Try it now

No setup: click a link, sign in to your free account if asked, and the finder starts by asking about your problem.

## What a session looks like

An abridged exchange, based on [worked case 1](docs/cases/01-chw-hypertension-visits.md):

> **You:** Our 10 health workers can make about 80 home visits a week across 1,200 hypertension patients. How do we decide who gets visited?
>
> **Finder:** Is a visit used up once given, or is the slot free again next week? And does a visit help everyone equally?
>
> **You:** It's free again next week. Some patients respond to a visit, some take their pills anyway, and some won't change no matter what. Adherence also slips on its own after a few weeks.
>
> *… three more questions: who chooses, how costly a wrong week is, and what data the workers record …*
>
> **Finder:** This is **Class 1: Dynamic State Evolution**, solved with a restless bandit. Each week, score every patient by how much a visit *now* improves their adherence over the coming weeks, and visit the top 80. A risk score would send visits to people who lapse whether visited or not. The health workers never see a score: the M&E officer exports one list per worker, "visit these 8 patients this week" …

## Why the method matters

Social-sector budgets almost never cover the need. In 2025, humanitarian appeals met only 35% of their funding target, the biggest funding gap in appeal history ([ALNAP, 2026](https://alnap.org/help-library/resources/sohs-2026/chapter-1-prioritisation-under-pressure/1-1-the-reality-of-funding-gaps/)). Every program is deciding who gets the call, the well, the patrol or the place, and those decisions are usually made with a ranked spreadsheet or a risk score.

The right method can do far better with the same budget, but "use AI" is not one thing. A prediction model tells you who is at risk; it doesn't tell you whom your visit will actually *help*, where poachers will move once you show up, or how to plan for a flood that looks nothing like the last one. Each of those is a different mathematical problem with its own tool, and most of these tools have already been tested in the field:

**1. Restless bandits: spend scarce contact time where it changes behavior.** ([Class 1: Dynamic State Evolution](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#class-1-dynamic-state-evolution)) *Which beneficiaries get a reminder call this week?* In ARMMAN's maternal-health program in India, a restless-bandit policy choosing which of about 23,000 enrolled mothers received a live call led to 32% fewer drops in engagement than standard care ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/using-ml-to-boost-engagement-with-a-maternal-and-child-health-program-in-india/)).

**2. Welfare optimization: allocate permanent resources fairly, and prove it.** ([Class 2: Static Demographic Targeting](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#class-2-static-demographic-targeting)) *Which villages get the new wells?* Integer programs with explicit fairness rules make allocations auditable. Panelot, a selection algorithm from Carnegie Mellon and Harvard researchers that evens out each volunteer's chance of being chosen, had been used to select more than 40 citizens' assemblies by 2021 ([Hertz Foundation](https://www.hertzfoundation.org/news/a-better-citizens-assembly-bailey-flanigan/)). A related approach, targeting with machine learning on phone data, cut the number of eligible poor people left out of Togo's emergency cash transfers by 4–21% compared with geographic targeting ([Aiken et al., Nature 2022](https://poverty-action.org/publication/machine-learning-and-phone-data-can-improve-targeting-humanitarian-aid)).

**3. Security games: patrol against adversaries who adapt.** ([Class 3: Spatiotemporal Security & Adaptive Search](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#class-3-spatiotemporal-security--adaptive-search)) *Where should rangers patrol when poachers move?* Randomized, game-theoretic schedules stay effective because they cannot be learned and avoided. In Uganda's Queen Elizabeth National Park, rangers who patrolled two rarely visited areas that the PAWS model flagged as high-risk found numerous snares and other signs of poaching ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/this-ai-hunts-poachers)); the same family of models has scheduled US Coast Guard patrols in Boston since 2011 ([AI Magazine](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/aimagazine/article/view/2401)).

**4. Robust optimization: prepare for disasters you can't forecast.** ([Class 4: Robust Contingency Planning](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#class-4-robust-contingency-planning)) *Where should supplies wait before an unpredictable flood?* When there are only a handful of past events and getting it wrong costs lives, robust models plan against the bad cases instead of the average one. This is the least field-proven class: the evidence is mostly in models, including studies of the WFP-managed UN Humanitarian Response Depot network ([Stienen et al., Omega 2021](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/67452240/1_s2.0_S0305048321001031_main.pdf)). The assistant says so plainly.

**5. Matching markets: respect choice on both sides.** ([Class 5: Strategic Matching Markets](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#class-5-strategic-matching-markets)) *How should students be assigned to schools when families and schools both have preferences?* When New York City redesigned its high-school admissions around deferred acceptance, the number of students assigned to a school they had not chosen fell from about 30,000 to about 3,000 in the first year ([Abdulkadiroğlu, Pathak & Roth, AER 2005](https://stanford.edu/~alroth/papers/nycAEAPP.pdf)). The National Kidney Registry's paired-exchange matching had facilitated more than 4,600 living-donor kidney transplants by 2021 ([Osbun et al., Am J Transplant](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8720056)).

**6. Participatory budgeting: let the community split the budget.** ([Class 6: Participatory Allocation](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#class-6-participatory-allocation)) *How should a neighborhood fund decide which projects get built?* Voting rules decide whose priorities win. Boston's 2016 youth participatory budget used knapsack voting, in which each voter builds a bundle that fits the budget ([Goel et al., ACM TEAC 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.06856)). In Aarau, Switzerland, counting the 2023 vote by the method of equal shares funded 17 projects instead of the 7 a standard count would have chosen with the same budget ([Pournaras et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14349)).

**7. Effect-based targeting: serve the people the program actually helps.** ([Class 7: Effect-Based Targeting](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#class-7-effect-based-targeting)) *Who should get the nudge, the grant or the training place?* The neediest are not always the people a program helps most. In a field experiment with more than 53,000 CUNY students, targeting financial-aid reminders by estimated effect beat targeting by predicted need, and nudging the students with the lowest predicted outcomes actually hurt ([Athey, Keleher & Spiess, J. Econometrics 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.08672)).

**8. Adaptive experiments: learn which option works while still serving people.** ([Class 8: Learn-While-Allocating](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#class-8-learn-while-allocating)) *Which of three job-search supports should we offer?* Instead of splitting people evenly for a year, an adaptive design shifts people toward the options that are working. The International Rescue Committee funded such an experiment for Syrian refugees and Jordanian jobseekers; the gain from adapting was small, about one percentage point of employment, but it showed cash grants mattered most for refugees ([Caria et al., JEEA 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvad067)).

**9. Routing and logistics: move goods at least cost.** ([Class 9: Routing & Logistics](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#class-9-routing--logistics)) *Which warehouse should supply which camp, and by which route?* The World Food Programme's Optimus model designs food baskets, sourcing and delivery together; in Iraq it cut costs by 12% for the same nutrition, and WFP's analytics work won the 2021 INFORMS Edelman Award ([Peters et al., INFORMS J. Optimization 2021](https://doi.org/10.1287/ijoo.2019.0047)). Optimized school-bus routes saved Boston Public Schools about $5 million a year ([Bertsimas, Delarue & Martin, PNAS 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1811462116)).

**10. Online assignment: place people and items as they arrive.** ([Class 10: Online Assignment & Waitlists](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#class-10-online-assignment--waitlists)) *A refugee family arrives today; which city should it go to, when more families are coming?* The resettlement agency HIAS uses Annie MOORE, which predicts where each family is likely to find work and places families within each office's yearly quota; backtests suggested a 22–38% gain in short-run employment ([Ahani et al., Operations Research 2021](https://doi.org/10.1287/opre.2020.2093)).

**11. Network seeding: train the people who will spread the word.** ([Class 11: Network Seeding](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#class-11-network-seeding)) *We can train 20 peer leaders; which 20?* In a trial with 713 homeless youth in Los Angeles, HIV risk behavior fell in the group whose peer leaders were chosen by an algorithm that maps who knows whom ([Wilder et al., AAAI 2021](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v35i17.17754)). In Haryana, India, sending immunization reminders through villagers nominated as good spreaders of news raised vaccinations ([Banerjee et al., Review of Economic Studies 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdz008)).

**12. Risk-based inspection: look where the hidden problems are.** ([Class 12: Risk-Based Inspection](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#class-12-risk-based-inspection)) *Which homes should we check for lead pipes?* Models that rank homes by predicted risk and learn from each inspection guided the search for lead service lines in Flint, Michigan ([Abernethy et al., KDD 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.10692)). Greece's Eva system chose which arriving travelers to test for COVID-19 and found about 1.85 times as many asymptomatic infections as random testing ([Bastani et al., Nature 2021](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-04014-z)).

Choosing the wrong class wastes money and can do harm: visits pile up on people who would have done fine anyway, patrols become predictable, or a placement scheme drives away the people it was meant to serve. Sometimes the honest answer is that a simple rule is enough, and the assistant will say that too. Every recommendation also names the best paper, spreadsheet or calculator alternative to the method, says in what ways it comes close (total outcome, fairness, worst case, quality of evidence) and lists the assumptions to check before settling for it. Some programs need two methods together, such as a forecast trigger that decides when to pay and a targeting rule that decides who; the assistant asks about a second decision and names a primary class and a layer when there is one.

See the [**worked cases**](docs/cases/) for sixteen examples across health, water, conservation, disaster preparedness, youth employment, food security, civic participation, resettlement and environmental health: one for each class, a "no model" case, and three cases that combine two classes. Each also walks through the follow-ups after the recommendation, from narrowing down tools and partners to designing a pilot. To understand how each method works, read the [**tutorials**](docs/tutorials/): one per single-class case, assuming only basic statistics, each with a runnable program that also measures the simpler alternative against the method.

**After you've picked an approach**, the [**implementation playbook**](prompt/knowledge/playbook.md) covers what comes next: fitting the model to your program, readiness checks, pilot design, tools and pro bono partners, further reading, and a one-page brief to send to a technical partner. The assistant offers each of these as a follow-up, tailored to your answers.

## Quick start

Pick your platform. Every file you need is in [`dist/`](dist/), and each [release](https://github.com/ashitpatel/ai4sgfinder/releases/latest) attaches the same files as downloads, so you don't need to clone the repository.

### Claude

**Claude Project** (claude.ai, any plan with Projects)
1. Create a Project named *AI4SG Finder*.
2. Paste [`dist/instructions.md`](dist/instructions.md) into **Project instructions**.
3. Upload [`dist/taxonomy.md`](dist/taxonomy.md) and [`dist/playbook.md`](dist/playbook.md) to **Project knowledge**.
4. Start a chat in the Project and describe your problem.

**Claude Skill** (claude.ai or Claude Desktop)
- **Settings → Capabilities → Skills → Upload skill**, and choose `ai4sg-finder.zip` from the [latest release](https://github.com/ashitpatel/ai4sgfinder/releases/latest) (also at [`dist/ai4sg-finder.zip`](dist/ai4sg-finder.zip)).

**Claude Code plugin**

This repository is also a Claude Code plugin marketplace. In Claude Code, run:
```
/plugin install ai4sg-finder --marketplace ashitpatel/ai4sgfinder
```
On versions of Claude Code without the one-step form, add the marketplace first:
```
/plugin marketplace add ashitpatel/ai4sgfinder
/plugin install ai4sg-finder@ai4sgfinder
```
Then type `/ai4sg-finder:finder`, or just ask Claude for help choosing an allocation method. Updates arrive through `/plugin` when a new version is released.

### ChatGPT (Custom GPT)
1. In ChatGPT, open **Explore GPTs → Create → Configure**.
2. Paste [`dist/instructions.md`](dist/instructions.md) into **Instructions** (it is kept under the 8,000-character limit).
3. Under **Knowledge**, upload [`dist/taxonomy.md`](dist/taxonomy.md) and [`dist/playbook.md`](dist/playbook.md).
4. Suggested conversation starter: *"I run a program and need help deciding how to allocate a limited resource."*

### Gemini (Gem)
1. In Gemini, open **Gems → New Gem**.
2. Paste [`dist/instructions.md`](dist/instructions.md) into **Instructions**.
3. Under **Knowledge**, add [`dist/taxonomy.md`](dist/taxonomy.md) and [`dist/playbook.md`](dist/playbook.md).

### Google AI Studio
1. Open [aistudio.google.com](https://aistudio.google.com) and start a new chat prompt.
2. Paste [`dist/all-in-one.md`](dist/all-in-one.md) into **System instructions**. It contains the instructions and the reference together, so nothing needs uploading.
3. Pick a Gemini model, type a first message about your problem, and save the prompt so you can reopen or share it.

### Browser assistants with a custom-instructions field
Assistants such as Perplexity Spaces let you save instructions and attach files. Paste [`dist/instructions.md`](dist/instructions.md) as the instructions and upload [`dist/taxonomy.md`](dist/taxonomy.md) and [`dist/playbook.md`](dist/playbook.md) as files. If there is no file upload, paste [`dist/all-in-one.md`](dist/all-in-one.md) as the instructions instead, provided the field accepts it (about 35,000 characters).

### Any browser assistant, no setup
For assistants with no place for custom instructions, such as Google AI Mode in Search, Gemini in Chrome, Microsoft Copilot, or a fresh chat in any app, paste [`dist/kickoff.md`](dist/kickoff.md) as your **first message**. It is a condensed version (under 4,000 characters, so it fits most chat boxes) that asks the assistant to take on the role and start the interview.

The kickoff is shorter than the full prompt and carries only the core verified references, so expect less detailed evidence sections. It still offers the six follow-ups, but without the playbook they rely on the assistant's general knowledge; for tools, partners and reading, check the [playbook](prompt/knowledge/playbook.md) yourself. Assistants without saved instructions can also drift out of the role in a long chat; paste it again if that happens.

## Editing the prompt

Edit only the files under `prompt/` (and `VERSION` when releasing), then regenerate:

```bash
python3 scripts/build.py
```

`kickoff.md` is condensed by hand, so when you change the full prompt, update it to match. The build fails if the kickoff is missing any filter or recommendation heading from `system-prompt.md`, if it exceeds 4,000 characters, or if the instructions exceed the 8,000-character Custom GPT limit. After a substantive prompt change, run the [worked cases](docs/cases/README.md#using-the-cases) and check the results. CI runs `python3 scripts/build.py --check` and fails when the generated files were not regenerated after an edit.

## Releasing

1. Update [`VERSION`](VERSION) and add a matching `## <version> — <date>` section to [`CHANGELOG.md`](CHANGELOG.md).
2. Run `python3 scripts/build.py`. This writes the new version into the plugin manifest, which is how Claude Code users get the update.
3. Optionally run `claude plugin validate --strict .` to check the plugin and marketplace.
4. Commit, then tag and push:
   ```bash
   git tag v$(cat VERSION) && git push origin main v$(cat VERSION)
   ```

The [release workflow](.github/workflows/release.yml) checks that the tag matches `VERSION` and that the generated files are current, then publishes a GitHub release with the changelog section as its notes and the `dist/` files attached.

## How the assistant works

1. **A two-stage interview**, one question per turn. Five filters (the resource and its intent; who holds choice; whether anyone avoids or games the program; tolerance for being wrong; and field data) place the problem in one of five families. One or two narrowing questions then pick the class within the family, and a last question asks whether the program has a second decision (when to act, where to look first, how it reaches people, or which version works for whom) that calls for a second class as a layer. The questions are listed in the [decision tree](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#2-the-ngo-decision-tree); the combinations seen in practice are in [When two classes combine](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#5-when-two-classes-combine).
2. **A six-part recommendation**: the recommended class, a counterfactual warning (what fails with a naive ML model or spreadsheet), a field deployment pathway using paper grids or Excel, real deployment evidence, a report card of successes, challenges, and unknowns, and the simpler alternative with how close it comes and what to check ([Simpler alternatives for each class](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md#4-simpler-alternatives-for-each-class)), ending with next steps.
3. **Six optional follow-ups**, drawn from `playbook.md` and tailored to your answers: fit the model to your program, readiness checks, pilot design, build options (tools and partners), reading and people to contact, and a one-page brief for a technical partner.
4. **Evidence integrity rules**: the assistant must not invent citations, tools or organizations, must prefer the verified references in `taxonomy.md` and `playbook.md`, and must label anything uncertain as unverified.

## Changes from the original draft

The first draft ([`docs/original-draft.md`](docs/original-draft.md)) was restructured as follows.

- **Added a filter for choice and preferences.** None of the original four filters could identify Class 5 (matching markets), so the assistant could never reach it.
- **Resolved "exactly one question at a time."** Filter 1 asked three questions at once. Each filter is now one turn, with its numbered prompts treated as optional guidance.
- **Added a "no model needed" outcome**, in line with the goal of shielding NGOs from fragile tech stacks.
- **Tied data feasibility to the recommendation.** Restless bandits need repeated observations per person; if the field can't supply them, the assistant now says so.
- **Added evidence integrity rules.** The draft asked for "documented deployments" and "literature tracking data" without guarding against fabricated citations, which language models produce readily.
- **Corrected the deployment examples.** Several attributions in the original taxonomy could not be verified or were misattributed. They are listed under *Unverified claims* in [`taxonomy.md`](prompt/knowledge/taxonomy.md), and the assistant is told not to cite them as fact. Verified references were added for each class.
- **Renamed Class 4** from "Robust Contingency Matching" to "Robust Contingency Planning," since it is not a matching method.

## Limitations

- The assistant recommends an *approach*; it does not build the model. Implementation still needs an operations-research or ML practitioner.
- Language models can still state inaccurate facts despite the integrity rules. Check every citation against its primary source before using it in a proposal or publication.
- The verified reference list reflects published work as of 2026 and is not exhaustive. Contributions with full citations are welcome.
