# Where AI4SG Finder Fits

Many organizations help NGOs and governments use AI for social good. They evaluate AI tools, fund nonprofits to build them, advise and train, or build solutions with partners. This page compares fourteen of them with AI4SG Finder, using what each one says on its own website.

**In short:** none of the fourteen publishes a tool for choosing which kind of AI method fits a program's problem. Their closest material does one of five things:

- asks whether AI is needed at all;
- helps choose among large language models (LLMs), or asks applicants to justify generative AI without saying how to compare it with other methods;
- catalogues kinds of AI use for inspiration, without saying which fits a given problem;
- sets out a project process, from scoping to deployment, that leaves the choice of method to the team;
- lists the risks of one kind of AI tool.

Optimization, the family of methods for allocating something scarce, is also nearly absent: a handful of projects name it, and no guidance or catalogue treats it as a category.

AI4SG Finder covers the step before building, funding or evaluating: it helps one program work out what kind of decision it faces and which family of methods fits, including when no model is needed. It complements these organizations rather than competing with them.

## How the organizations were chosen

The comparison follows the path a program takes from an idea to a tested tool:

1. **Choose** the method. This is where AI4SG Finder fits.
2. **Build** it, with a technical partner.
3. **Fund** it, through a grant or accelerator.
4. **Evaluate** it, to learn whether it works.

Around these, advisors and capacity builders help organizations get ready for AI.

We chose well-known organizations in each role that publish their projects or guidance, so their work can be checked. This is a sample, not a census. Other well-known organizations, such as IDinsight, Innovations for Poverty Action and Microsoft's AI for Good Lab, were not checked.

**Not compared:** providers of AI models and infrastructure, such as [AI4Bharat](https://ai4bharat.iitm.ac.in/) and [BharatGen](https://bharatgen.com/), which build open language, speech and vision models for others to build on. They supply building blocks rather than help programs choose a method, so comparing them on that would be unfair.

## How this was checked

Each claim comes from the organization's own website or a file it hosts, read in November 2026. Programs change, so check the linked pages for current details.

Counts by kind of AI follow each organization's own project descriptions. **Named** means the organization states the technique. **Inferred** means our reading of what it describes, for example a "chatbot" counted as generative AI when no model is named. Where a list was long, we counted a defined part of it, and say which.

## What the comparison shows

- **No method-selection tool.** None of the fourteen helps a program choose between, say, a prediction model, an optimization model, a matching mechanism or a generative assistant for a given decision. The closest items ask whether to use AI at all (J-PAL, Fast Forward, Pragati, NetHope, the McGovern Foundation), help choose among generative AI tools or uses (Fast Forward, DataKind, and a guide data.org links), ask applicants to justify generative AI (Google.org), catalogue kinds of use (the McGovern Foundation, data.org) or set out a project process (DataKind, Omdena).
- **Optimization is rare, and missing from the guidance.** Among roughly 190 projects and grants we classified, three name optimization as their main method: J-PAL's teacher placement in Rwanda, DataKind's sanitation routing in Haiti and Omdena's vehicle-routing case study. Two more Omdena projects list it as a secondary goal, DataKind's Playbook uses route optimization as a scoping example, and a keyword search of all 890 McGovern Foundation grants found a few more, such as a vaccine optimizer. None of the guidance or catalogues treats optimization or allocation as a category of AI use.
- **Not mostly chatbots.** Generative AI dominates Pragati's cohorts, Google.org's generative AI accelerator and Project Tech4Dev's AI work. Elsewhere the mix is wider: prediction leads at data.org and among NetHope's case studies, computer vision leads at Wadhwani AI, and J-PAL's projects are mixed (5 of 16 generative).

## How they fit together

A program could use AI4SG Finder to choose an approach, take that brief to a technical partner such as DataKind, Omdena or Wadhwani AI, apply for build funding from a program such as Fast Forward, Pragati or the McGovern Foundation, and test the result with an evaluation like those J-PAL funds. NetHope, data.org and Dasra build the sector's capacity around all of these steps.

## Not checked

These could not be read, so they may contain method-choice guidance or change the counts:

- Wadhwani AI's Responsible AI Playbook, a Google Drive file that does not allow downloads, and its Problem Framing guide, listed by title only.
- Google's older AI for Social Good guide, whose address now opens a general landing page.
- NetHope's directory of 170 AI-enabled humanitarian projects (a spreadsheet we did not classify), its gated guide to AI solutions for humanitarian nonprofits, and its member-only working-group pages.
- The rows of the McGovern Foundation's use-case library and parts of its problem-definition course.
- data.org's AI to Accelerate Inclusion impact report, a PDF too large to fetch.
- Omdena's projects and case studies beyond the first page shown, and DataKind's chapter projects.

Project classifications marked inferred are our judgment. Corrections are welcome as issues or pull requests.
