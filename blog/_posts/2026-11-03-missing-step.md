*Part 5 of 6 in a series on [AI4SG Finder](https://github.com/ashitpatel/ai4sgfinder).*

Who else is working on AI for social good, and where does AI4SG Finder fit? To answer that honestly, I looked at fourteen organizations doing serious work in this space, using only what each says on its own website and in the files it hosts. The full comparison, with a link for every claim, is on the project's [landscape page](https://github.com/ashitpatel/ai4sgfinder/blob/main/docs/landscape.md). This post summarizes it.

## Four roles

A program usually goes from an idea to a tested tool in four steps: choose a method, build it, fund it, and evaluate it. Advisors and capacity builders help organizations get ready along the way. The fourteen fall into these roles:

- **Evaluate.** J-PAL and CEGA test whether AI applications work. J-PAL's Project AI Evidence has funded 16 projects; CEGA's work includes machine-learning targeting of cash transfers in Togo.
- **Fund.** Fast Forward, Google.org, the Patrick J. McGovern Foundation and The/Nudge's Pragati program back nonprofits that build AI, through grants, accelerators and mentoring.
- **Advise and train.** NetHope, data.org and Dasra build the sector's readiness, through toolkits, courses, challenges and convening.
- **Build.** DataKind, Omdena, Digital Green, Wadhwani AI and Project Tech4Dev develop AI with NGOs and governments.

We chose well-known organizations in each role that publish their projects or guidance, so their work could be checked. It's a sample, not a census. Providers of AI models and infrastructure, which supply building blocks rather than help programs choose a method, were left out.

## The gap: no way to choose the kind of AI

None of the fourteen publishes a way to choose which kind of AI fits a program's problem: a prediction model, an optimization model, a matching mechanism or a generative assistant. Their closest material does something else:

- **It asks whether to use AI at all.** J-PAL's AI Evidence Playbook, NetHope's AI Suitability Toolkit and the McGovern Foundation's problem-definition course all help an organization decide whether AI is the right path, which is a valuable first question.
- **It helps choose among generative AI tools.** Fast Forward's playbook covers choosing among large language models; DataKind has a matrix for picking generative AI uses by risk and impact. Google.org's generative AI accelerator asks applicants to explain why generative AI beats the alternatives, without saying how to compare them.
- **It catalogues kinds of use.** The McGovern Foundation's use-case library sorts nonprofit examples into 12 categories, such as prediction, detection and recommendation; data.org classifies data uses as observing, reasoning or acting. Both are useful for inspiration, but neither says which kind fits a given problem.
- **It sets out a project process.** DataKind's Playbook and Omdena's guides take a project from scoping to deployment and leave the choice of method to the team.
- **It lists risks.** Digital Green's risk taxonomy for AI farm-advisory services covers risks from access to privacy, for one kind of tool.

This is the step AI4SG Finder covers. It asks what kind of decision a program faces and recommends a family of methods, or no model at all. A program can then take that recommendation to a technical partner, a funder or an evaluator.

## Optimization is nearly absent

One finding stood out. Many social programs come down to allocating something scarce: visits, patrols, places, supplies. Optimization is the family of methods built for exactly that. Yet among roughly 190 projects and grants these organizations list, only three name optimization as their main method:

- J-PAL's teacher placement project in Rwanda;
- DataKind's routing for a mobile sanitation service in Haiti;
- an Omdena case study on vehicle routing.

A few more mention it in passing, and a keyword search of all 890 McGovern Foundation grants found a handful, such as a vaccine optimizer. More telling, none of the guidance or catalogues treats optimization or allocation as a category of AI use. The McGovern Foundation's 12 categories leave it out, and DataKind's guide to choosing a technical approach never mentions it.

## Not mostly chatbots

It would be easy to assume these organizations mostly fund chatbots. The evidence doesn't support that. Generative AI dominates some programs, such as Pragati's cohorts, Google.org's generative AI accelerator and Project Tech4Dev's AI work. Elsewhere the mix is wider: prediction leads among data.org's challenge awardees and NetHope's case studies, computer vision leads among Wadhwani AI's solutions, and J-PAL's projects are mixed, with 5 of 16 generative.

The gap isn't that the sector ignores other kinds of AI. It's that no one helps a program decide which kind its problem needs.

## How they fit together

AI4SG Finder isn't a competitor to any of these organizations. It sits at the start of the path:

1. **Choose** an approach with AI4SG Finder, and get a one-page brief.
2. **Build** it with a technical partner such as DataKind, Omdena or Wadhwani AI.
3. **Fund** it through a program such as Fast Forward, Pragati or the McGovern Foundation.
4. **Evaluate** it, as J-PAL and CEGA do.

NetHope, data.org and Dasra build the sector's capacity around all of these steps.

## What we couldn't check

A few documents couldn't be read, so they may contain method-choice guidance: Wadhwani AI's Responsible AI Playbook and Problem Framing guide, NetHope's directory of 170 AI-enabled humanitarian projects and its gated guide to AI solutions, and parts of the McGovern Foundation's Learning Hub. The [landscape page](https://github.com/ashitpatel/ai4sgfinder/blob/main/docs/landscape.md) lists them all. Where a project's technique isn't stated, the classification is our judgment, and corrections are welcome.
