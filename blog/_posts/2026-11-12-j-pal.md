*Part 7 of 9 in a series on [AI4SG Finder](https://github.com/ashitpatel/ai4sgfinder). Parts 7 to 9 are about working with research partners.*

The [fifth post]({{ site.baseurl }}/missing-step/) placed AI4SG Finder at the start of a path: choose a method, build it, fund it, evaluate it. J-PAL and CEGA appeared at the end of that path, as evaluators. That undersells them. Both offer a great deal that a program can use long before anything is ready to evaluate.

This post covers J-PAL; the [next]({{ site.baseurl }}/cega/) covers CEGA, and the [last]({{ site.baseurl }}/research-partners/) shows how to use AI4SG Finder to approach either one. Everything here comes from J-PAL's own website, read in November 2026.

## What J-PAL is

The Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab was founded in 2003 in MIT's economics department by Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo and Sendhil Mullainathan. Its mission is to reduce poverty by making sure policy is informed by scientific evidence ([About J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/about-us)).

It is not a consultancy. It is a network of university researchers supported by professional staff:

- **Affiliated professors** at universities around the world, more than 390 of them, plus more than 765 invited researchers. They set their own research agendas and raise the funds for their studies ([2025 by the numbers](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/2025/by-the-numbers); [About J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/about-us)).
- **Staff** of more than 400 in research, policy, education and training, at a global office at MIT and **seven regional offices**: Africa, Europe, Latin America and the Caribbean, the Middle East and North Africa, North America, South Asia and Southeast Asia ([About J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/about-us)).
- **A close partner, Innovations for Poverty Action (IPA)**, which runs field research through its own country offices ([IPA](https://www.poverty-action.org/about)).

Its affiliates have run more than 2,500 randomized evaluations in 124 countries ([2025 by the numbers](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/2025/by-the-numbers)). A randomized evaluation compares people or places that get a program with similar ones that don't, chosen by lottery, so the difference in outcomes can be credited to the program.

## Upstream: before you build

Much of J-PAL's most useful material for an NGO is free to read and needs no partnership.

- **What has already been tested.** The [evaluations database](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluations) lists its affiliates' studies by sector and country. [Policy Insights](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/policy-insights) summarize the lessons across many studies on one question. Before designing an allocation rule, it's worth checking whether someone has already tested one like it.
- **The AI Evidence Playbook.** Published in February 2026 by J-PAL's [Project AI Evidence](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/initiative/project-ai-evidence-paie), the [playbook](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/ai-evidence-playbook) reviews what is known about AI in social programs. Its first pathway is improving program targeting. Two of its lessons matter for any allocation decision. Survey-based targeting is generally more accurate at finding poor households, while machine-learning targeting can be cheaper at national scale. And targeting people by expected impact rather than need can leave the poorest under-served.
- **A warning about results that don't reach outcomes.** The playbook describes a model in India that flagged fake firms for tax inspectors. Of the firms it flagged, 53% were fraudulent, against 38% under the existing rules. Yet tax collections didn't improve, partly because enforcement processes weren't adapted to act on the new information. A better list is not the same as a better outcome.
- **Research resources.** Guides to [randomized evaluation, power calculations and administrative data](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/research-resources), including the free [IDEA handbook](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/initiative/idea-handbook) on using administrative data for research while protecting privacy.
- **Training.** J-PAL runs [custom courses](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/page/custom-offerings) for governments, NGOs and foundations, from 3–5-day courses on evaluating social programs to 2–3-day evidence-to-policy workshops, in six languages. You request one from the global team or your regional office.

## Downstream: when you have a program to test

Once a program or tool exists, J-PAL's role is to find out whether it works. Here the main thing to understand is that **funding and studies go through researchers**, not directly to NGOs.

- **Project AI Evidence** funds randomized evaluations of AI tools in social programs. Its most recent call for proposals closed in early 2026. Applicants had to be J-PAL affiliates, invited researchers or eligible scholars, though proposals could include collaborators outside the network ([request for proposals](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/initiative/project-ai-evidence-rfp)).
- **Sector initiatives** in education, social protection, government innovation and others fund evaluations the same way. The Innovation in Government Initiative, for example, funds affiliates working with a government partner, or with an NGO delivering services through government systems.
- **One exception** is in the United States, where state and local governments can apply to the [LEVER program](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/initiative/state-and-local-innovation-initiative) for technical help, flexible funding and connections to researchers.

So for most NGOs, working with J-PAL means working with one of its researchers, usually through a regional office.

J-PAL's guidance to researchers on [choosing a partner](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/assessing-viability-and-building-relationships) is a good checklist from the other side. Researchers look for enough participants, the ability to collect data, willingness to build random assignment into operations, and a champion inside the organization. It also says plainly that a randomized evaluation may not be the right fit for every partner.

## Allocation decisions J-PAL has studied

J-PAL's work touches several of the twelve decisions in [Part 3]({{ site.baseurl }}/twelve-decisions/):

- **Who gets cash transfers** (Class 2). In Indonesia, researchers compared a formula based on household assets, community ranking and a mix of the two across 640 sub-villages. The formula had the lowest targeting error; community ranking left residents more satisfied ([evaluation](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/effectively-targeting-anti-poverty-programs-indonesia)).
- **Which teacher goes to which school.** In Rwanda, about 20% of primary teachers leave their posts each year, and teachers are placed at random within districts. A Project AI Evidence study is testing predictive modeling to improve placement ([project list](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/initiative-projects/38)).
- **Where inspectors look** (Class 12). The India tax example above.

## Where AI4SG Finder fits

The AI Evidence Playbook asks programs to say what they would do without AI, such as a simple decision rule or more staff. AI4SG Finder is built around the same question. It names the kind of decision a program faces, recommends a method or no model at all, and sets out the best simpler option to compare against.

Which one to start with depends on what you already know.

**Start with J-PAL when your question is about the program, not the method:**

- **You want to know what has already worked for a problem like yours**, such as how to target cash transfers or get teachers to stay. Search the [evaluations database](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluations) and [Policy Insights](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/policy-insights) before designing anything new.
- **You're weighing whether to use AI at all.** Read the [AI Evidence Playbook](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/ai-evidence-playbook), especially its section on targeting and its questions about what you would do without AI.
- **Your staff need to understand evaluation** before they can commission one. Ask your regional office about a custom course.
- **You run a large program that can be randomized or rolled out in stages, and you want to know whether it works.** Contact your regional office about an evaluation.

**Start with AI4SG Finder when you know what you allocate but not how to decide:**

- **You don't yet know which kind of method fits, or whether you need a model.** The interview names the decision, recommends one of twelve method families or a simple rule, and sets out the simpler option to beat.
- **You need to get from a recommendation to something staff can run.** The follow-ups cover fitting the model, readiness, a pilot, and build partners and tools. J-PAL doesn't build tools.
- **You're not ready for an evaluation.** If the program is small, can't be staged, or doesn't yet record its outcome, a pilot with a build partner comes first.

**Then move from one to the other.** After an AI4SG Finder recommendation, check J-PAL's evidence for tests of similar rules, use its research resources when designing the pilot, and, once the program passes the three-part test in [Part 9]({{ site.baseurl }}/research-partners/), send the one-page brief to your regional office. Going the other way, if J-PAL's evidence shows that better targeting or placement helps but doesn't tell you which method suits your own decision, AI4SG Finder can help you choose one and compare it with a simple rule.

What AI4SG Finder doesn't do is evaluate anything. It recommends; only a well-designed test shows whether the recommendation helped.
